Most hosting comparison pages show a very low monthly price. It is a real price, in the sense that someone can pay it. But look closer and you will usually find three conditions attached, and together they decide what you actually pay over the life of the site.

This is not a trick that one company plays. It is how most of the shared hosting market is priced. Once you know the pattern you can read any pricing page in about a minute.

## The price needs a long prepay

The headline number is often only available if you pay for two, three or four years in one go. The page says "$4.49 a month", and the checkout asks for well over a hundred dollars today.

Choose monthly billing and the same plan can cost several times more, if monthly billing is offered at all. So the first thing to find is the term: how many months do you have to buy to get the price in the big type?

A long prepay is not bad in itself. It is bad when you have not yet learned whether the host is any good. You are locked in before the first slow day.

## The renewal price is much higher

When the first term ends, the plan renews at its "regular" price. This number is always published somewhere, usually in small grey type under the button or behind a "see renewal prices" link.

On popular shared and entry-level cloud plans, public list prices show renewal rates of roughly **three to four times** the intro price. A plan that cost under five dollars a month in the first term can renew at around seventeen. Nothing about the service changed. Only the price did.

The intro price is a discount on the first invoice. The renewal price is the price.

> A fair way to compare two hosts is to ignore the first term completely and compare what year three costs.

## The work is still yours

Cheap plans are usually self-managed. That means the host keeps the server switched on, and everything above that is your job:

- applying updates, and fixing the site when an update breaks it
- checking that backups exist and can actually be restored
- sorting out SSL certificates that fail to renew
- moving the site in, and moving it out again later
- working out why the site is slow this week

If you have a developer, this is their time, and their time has a price. If you do not, it is your evenings. Either way it belongs in the comparison. A plan that costs ten dollars less and takes two hours of someone's month is not cheaper.

## Seats and meters

Developer platforms price differently, and they have their own version of the same surprise.

The plan is charged **per person** who needs to deploy. A team of three pays three times the headline number. On top of that sits a usage meter for bandwidth, requests, build minutes or function time. Most months you stay inside the included amount. The month you get noticed, or the month a bot hammers your site, you find out what the meter costs.

These platforms are excellent at what they do. The point is only that the number on the pricing page is the floor, not the bill.

## What to check before you buy

A short list you can run against any host, including us:

1. **The renewal price**, not the intro price. Multiply it by 12 for the true yearly cost.
2. **The term** needed to get the advertised price, and what monthly billing costs instead.
3. **What "managed" means.** Who applies updates? Who restores a backup? How quickly, and is it included?
4. **Per-seat fees**, if anyone besides you needs a login.
5. **Usage meters** for bandwidth, requests or build minutes, and what happens when you go over.
6. **The way out.** Can you take a full copy of your site and leave in the middle of a term?

Write the answers down next to each other. The cheapest-looking option is rarely the cheapest by the end of the list.

## A worked comparison

Take a small business with one WordPress site and two people who need access.

On a typical shared plan the first two years look very cheap, as long as you prepay them. From year three the plan costs three to four times as much, and the updates, backups and fixes were yours the whole time.

On a managed plan with a fixed price, year one costs more than the shared plan's intro offer. Year three costs the same as year one. The updates, backups and fixes were never yours.

Which one is better depends on what your time is worth and how much you enjoy server administration. The only wrong way to choose is by the number in the biggest type.
